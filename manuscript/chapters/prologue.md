# Prologue — The Ember That Wouldn't Die

The light came down over Luminara the way it always did in the last hour of the day — thick and golden, pouring between the cliffside houses, setting the sea below the city alight until the water looked like a second, broken sun. In the Knightley forge, the light had competition. The forge-fire kept its own small sun banked in the hearth, and seven-year-old Damion liked the hour when the two suns argued through the open door about which of them owned the room.

He was supposed to be sorting rivets. That was the rule, and it was not a complicated rule: behind the counter, hands in the rivet tray, nowhere near the hearth. His father had made the rule. His mother enforced it the way she enforced everything, by simply knowing, the way weather knows where the cliffs are.

"Damion."

"I'm sorting," he said, and sorted faster, which was the same as confessing.

Elena Knightley did not look up from the steel she was tempering, but her mouth did something that was almost a smile. She worked the blade through the quench with the patience of a woman who had never once hurried anything into being what it was not. "Sort slower, then. You're frightening them."

He grinned into the tray. The rivets were warm from the room. Everything in the forge was warm — the stone, the tools, his skin, the air that tasted of iron and coal smoke and the faint green smell of the sea climbing the cliff. He had been born into this warmth the way other children were born into houses, and he could no more imagine a cold world than he could imagine a silent one.

On the inside of his wrist there was a mark, so faint that most days even he forgot it was there. A pale feathering under the brown of his skin, no bigger than his thumbnail, shaped — if you tilted it toward the light and squinted the way he had taught himself to squint — like a single feather, lying still. The other children had asked about it the summer before, the way children ask about anything they cannot have explained to them in one breath.

*What's it for?*

*Nothing,* his mother had said, too quickly, and then, softer, as if correcting herself in front of a guest: *He was born with it. That's all. Some people are born with a song in them. He was born with a feather.*

His father, Marcus, had said nothing at all, which was how Damion had learned that the mark was the kind of thing his father did not say nothing about unless it mattered.

So the mark was nothing. It did not glow. It did not speak. It lay under his skin like a word nobody had ever read aloud, and most days Damion believed his mother, because believing his mother was the easiest thing in the world.

That afternoon, an ember came loose from the hearth.

It was not supposed to happen. Embers died in the hearth or were drowned in the quench; they did not rise. But this one rose — a single coal-bright spark lifting out of the fire on the heat, drifting over the anvil, past his father's shoulder, slowing as it came, the way a leaf slows when the wind changes its mind. It crossed the forge at the height of a child's reaching hand, and it burned, impossibly, without falling.

Damion watched it come the way he watched storms come over the sea wall: with his whole face.

Later, no one would be able to say whether he reached for it because it was beautiful, or because it had slowed, or because some part of him — the part that lay still under his skin in the shape of a feather — had recognized it. Later, he would not be able to say it himself. He only knew that the ember was the most alive thing he had ever seen, and that it was coming to him the way a dog comes to the one person in the room it has chosen, and that his hand was already open.

*Damion—*

The ember settled into his palm like it had been carried there.

For one breath, it did not hurt. For one breath it only sat in the cup of his hand, brighter than the forge, brighter than the door, turning his fingers to dark glass around a captive star, and he felt — not pain, not yet — a *welcome*, deep in his wrist, where the faint mark stirred the way a sleeper stirs at the sound of their own name.

Then the ember broke, and kissed his sleeve, and the world caught fire.

The flames did not climb the way fire climbs in stories, all at once and roaring. They crept. They took the cuff first, curious, tasting the wool, and then they understood what they had found and they ran — up his arm in a bright, silent line, faster than his voice, which came out of him a whole second later in a scream that belonged to someone else. He beat at his arm with his other hand and only taught the fire new places to go. The heat reached his elbow. It reached for his wrist.

It reached the mark.

And the mark — the nothing, the feather, the word nobody had read aloud — *opened*.

There was no sound. There was a pressure, as if the forge, the cliff, the sea below and the whole golden sky had leaned in at once to look at him. The fire on his arm stopped climbing. It did not go out. It turned — every tongue of it bending toward his wrist like grass bending toward a dropped stone's ring in still water — and poured *inward*, into the feather under his skin, which drank the flames the way the dry summer earth drinks the first rain, and lit from within, and burned, at last, the way it had apparently always meant to burn: crimson first, then gold, then a color that had no business existing in a blacksmith's shop at the end of an ordinary day.

Damion stopped screaming. That frightened his mother more than the screaming had.

His father's cloak took the rest of the fire — good heavy wool, thrown and wrapped and beaten down with hands that had never once, in Damion's whole life, shaken. Marcus Knightley smothered the flames against his own chest and held his son inside the smoking dark of that cloak and said, "I've got you, I've got you, I've got you," like a man arguing with the world, and the world, for once, conceded.

But the arm was burned. When Elena pulled the cloth away with hands that had gone very steady and very slow, the skin of his forearm was blistered red-black almost to the elbow, and she made a sound then that Damion had never heard her make, a sound with no words in it at all, and it was that sound — not the pain, which had gone somewhere far away and merciful — that he would remember for the rest of his life.

They carried him through the golden streets as the light failed. Someone ran ahead. Someone else ran for the Order. The infirmary of the Order of the Emblematic stood at the top of the cliff road, white stone and tall windows, a place Luminara's children were taught to respect and never to enter, and Damion was carried in through its doors past robed figures who stepped aside with the particular silence of people who have just understood that an ordinary day is over.

The healers worked with poultices that smelled of rain and crushed green things, of the cold places between the stones of the world. The salves were Emblem-worked, and they did what they were made to do: they kept the corruption out. But the burn would not cool. All night it held its heat like a banked coal holds its heart, and the fever took him down into a dark where the forge followed him.

In the fever, he was seven and he was burning and there was no one in the room — his mother's voice came from very far away, reading his name off a list of the lost. In the fever, a great slow wingbeat sounded somewhere behind the walls of the world, once, twice, patient as a tide. In the fever, the pain in his arm gathered itself, drew together, became a single point of warmth that pulsed when his heart pulsed — and then, from within, in the deepest hour of the night, the wound did what no poultice had asked it to do.

It cauterized itself.

The healers found him at dawn sleeping the clean sleep of a child who has come through a fever, his temperature broken, his arm — his arm whole-skinned and smooth where ruin had been promised, marked only along the forearm with a feather of fine bright scar-lines, elegant as script, that had not been drawn by any hand. And on his wrist, where the pale nothing of a birthmark had lain all his life, the feather now stood in crimson and gold, faint but certain, like an ember seen through ash.

It pulsed, once, with his heartbeat. Then again. Then it settled into the rhythm of him and did not stop.

He woke to his mother's face above him, worn to the bone with a night of fear, and his father's great scarred hand wrapped entirely around his unburned one, and beyond them, standing at the foot of the bed in the gray infirmary light, a man he did not know.

The stranger was tall and weathered the way cliffs are weathered, with a Keeper's gray at his temples and eyes that had the stillness of deep water. He was not looking at the healers. He was not looking at the bandages or the salve-pots. He was looking at Damion's wrist, and he had been looking at it, Damion understood with the perfect clarity of childhood, for a long time.

"Arden," Elena said. Her voice had its steadiness back, but it was the steadiness of a drawn blade now. "He's a child. Whatever you're seeing — he's a *child*."

"I see a child," the man said mildly. "That's the trouble, Elena. It's never only that."

Marcus stood. He did not raise his voice; the whole room simply made space for the fact of him. "You've tended him. We're grateful. Now you'll tell me plain what it is you're not saying."

Arden Veylor looked at the blacksmith for a moment, and something crossed his face that might, in a lesser man, have been pity. "Plainly, then. Fire doesn't answer children, Marcus. It answers *itself*. What woke in your son last night isn't a talent and it isn't a curse, and I'd be lying if I told you I knew the whole of what it is. Keep him close to the forge. Keep him closer to you." His gaze went back to the wrist, to the feather pulsing its slow light against the boy's skin. "And when it wakes the rest of the way — and it will — send for me before you send for anyone else."

He left before the sun was fully up, and did not give them his reasons, and some part of Damion — the part that had opened like an eye in the fire — understood even then that the man had told them everything except the thing that mattered.

His parents never spoke of that morning in front of him again. But his mother, for years afterward, would catch herself watching his wrist when she thought he was asleep, and his father took to leaving the forge door open at all hours, even in winter, as if some part of him had decided that whatever had come for his son once might come again, and would find the way lit.

Damion healed. He grew. The scar-feather faded to a fine bright tracery that the other children decided was splendid and then, being children, forgot. He learned the rivets, then the tongs, then the hammer's grammar. And the ember that had started it all — the ember that wouldn't die — went quiet in him, banked down to almost nothing, a warmth in the wrist that pulsed when his heart pulsed and otherwise let him be.

Almost nothing.

Because some nights, lying awake while the sea worried the cliffs below Luminara and the forge cooled into the dark, Damion Knightley would press his thumb to the inside of his wrist and feel it there — steady as a second heart, patient as a tide behind a wall — the small, sleeping fire that had once answered him out of the air.

It was waiting. He did not yet have the words for what it was waiting for.

Neither did the world.
